## 1. What a second reorg teaches the organization

**Pillar:** Organizational Systems & Change Psychology · **Guardrails:** clean (307 words) · **Calendar row:** Jan 2027

> An organization learns from a restructure whether the last one was finished. When a second reorg arrives within eighteen months of the first, most people have only just rebuilt the working relationships the first one broke: who to call for a pricing exception, which manager will push back on a deadline, where a decision actually gets made. The new chart erases that map before it has settled.
>
> What people take from it is a rule about time. Structures here are provisional, so investing in them is a bad bet. The sensible response is to keep the old informal routes alive, commit lightly to the new reporting lines, and wait. Leaders read this as resistance. It is accurate learning from what the organization has seen.
>
> The cost shows up later. The first restructure usually bought a period of goodwill: people assumed the design was considered and gave it a fair trial. The second one is judged against the first. If the first never delivered what was promised, the second inherits that deficit before it has done anything.
>
> Before the design work starts, we ask a leader weighing another restructure to answer a few questions in writing. What did the last reorg promise, and was it measured? Which of the problems now on the table are structural, and which are the residue of an organization still absorbing the last change? Who in the middle of the organization will rebuild their working relationships for the second time, and what did it cost them the first time?
>
> When the answers point to an unfinished first change, the stronger move is often to finish it. Name what it was meant to fix, give each gap an owner, and allow it the twelve months it never had. A third structure laid over two half-built ones teaches the organization the same lesson, more firmly.

**The claim that rests on the firm's own observation:** "Before the design work starts, we ask a leader weighing another restructure to answer a few questions in writing."

- [ ] "most people have only just rebuilt" and "The first restructure usually bought a period of goodwill" are general claims that rest only on the firm's experience. Keep them if they match yours.

## 2. The hallway version of the decision

**Pillar:** Communication, Influence & Relational Leadership · **Guardrails:** clean (301 words) · **Calendar row:** Feb 2027

> Every announced decision has a second version, and it is the one most of the organization acts on. It forms in the hour after the meeting, when the people who were in the room explain to their own teams what was decided and why. Each retelling is honest. Each is also shaped by what the teller argued for and how much of the reasoning they accepted.
>
> The mechanism is simple. An executive who lost the argument in the room rarely repeats the reasons that beat them. They report the outcome, add a note of their own doubt, and let their team read the rest. By the time the decision reaches the front line, it carries the reservations of whoever relayed it. Nobody lied. The decision was translated by people with a stake in it.
>
> Senior leaders usually respond by announcing more widely: a memo, an all-hands, a recorded message. These settle the facts and do little for the meaning, because the front line still asks its own manager what the memo really means, and the manager's answer is the version that sticks.
>
> What works better happens before the announcement. Ask each executive who will relay the decision to say, in the room, how they will explain it to their team, including the reason it went against what they wanted, if it did. You hear the hallway version while you can still correct it. The executive also hears themselves commit to the reasoning, which is a stronger thing than consenting to the outcome.
>
> The check comes a week later. Ask a manager two levels down why the decision was made. If the answer matches the reasoning given in the room, the decision traveled intact. If it matches the reasoning of the person who argued against it, another memo will not fix that.

**The claim that rests on the firm's own observation:** This note has no "we" sentence. Its central claim is unattributed: "An executive who lost the argument in the room rarely repeats the reasons that beat them." The same goes for "Senior leaders usually respond by announcing more widely." Both are presented as general findings, and the firm's own experience is the only thing behind them.

**Check or soften:**
- [ ] Does "rarely repeats the reasons that beat them" match what you have seen? If it does, think about anchoring it as the firm's observation ("In our work, the executive who lost the argument..."). The other two notes are anchored that way.
- [ ] "It forms in the hour after the meeting" is a specific timing claim. Keep it or loosen it.
- [ ] "What works better" is a practice recommendation that no example supports. Say whether Metis has used the "say it in the room first" step with a client.

## 3. Choosing a successor is choosing a strategy

**Pillar:** Strategic Thinking & Decision Architecture · **Guardrails:** clean (285 words) · **Calendar row:** Mar 2027

> A succession decision is a strategy decision made in the language of people. A board that picks a successor before it agrees on what the next phase of the business requires ends up choosing on familiarity, and familiarity favors the candidate who most resembles the current CEO.
>
> The resemblance is easy to mistake for readiness. The internal candidate has run the largest division, knows the board, and has been trusted with the hardest problems of the current strategy. Those are real qualifications for the strategy the company has. Whether they fit the one it needs depends on a question the board has often left open: are the next five years about protecting a position or changing it?
>
> The two call for different leaders. Protecting a position rewards operating discipline and a steady hand with the existing executive team. Changing it rewards someone willing to move capital away from the businesses that made the current team's reputations, and to absorb the friction that follows. A board that has not said which one it is asking for will default to protection, because protection is what the outgoing CEO models every day.
>
> We ask boards to write the strategic brief before they write the candidate profile. One page: what the company must stop doing, what it must start, and which current strengths the next CEO is expected to wind down. The profile then follows from the brief, and each candidate is assessed against the work ahead.
>
> The brief also protects the person chosen. A CEO appointed to change course, without the board having said so in writing, meets the first resistance alone, and finds the board's patience tied to results the old strategy was never built to produce.

**The claim that rests on the firm's own observation:** "We ask boards to write the strategic brief before they write the candidate profile. One page: ..."

**Check or soften:**
- [ ] Does Metis advise boards on CEO succession and use a one-page brief? If not, change it to "A board should write..." so it doesn't present a service the firm doesn't offer.
- [ ] "will default to protection" is stated as a certainty. Soften it to "tends to default" unless you have seen it happen consistently.
